The Recognition Gap

How Skilled-Migration Systems Miss Prospective Talent
Gita Gabriella · Independent Research Project, 2026
Abstract

In 2025, Taiwan became a super-aged society. Like Japan, South Korea, and other economies facing workforce pressure associated with population ageing, it has increasingly turned to talent attraction as one policy response. This paper examines how skilled-migration systems recognise professional value, beginning with Taiwan and testing the resulting argument through comparison with Canada. Across the high-skilled pathways examined, eligibility relies predominantly on completed and verifiable forms of attainment, such as prior salary, credentials, and accumulated professional experience. These indicators provide administratively legible evidence of what an applicant has already achieved, but they are less able to recognise prospective value: the plausible but not-yet-resolved trajectory of someone who already possesses relevant professional capabilities but whose development has not yet crystallised into the markers required for formal recognition. This paper calls the resulting blind spot the recognition gap.

Canada provides a deliberately different institutional comparison. Although the two systems differ in which forms of completed attainment they recognise, where those attainments are accumulated, and how they become migration-relevant, both ultimately require an advancing trajectory to resolve into observable evidence before it becomes legible to the state. The comparison therefore identifies retrospective screening as a plausible common mechanism behind the recognition gap rather than treating the gap as a peculiarity of Taiwan. The paper further distinguishes two potential downstream costs: a leak, where mobile applicants redirect their effort elsewhere before becoming legible to the destination, and a trap, where constrained exit options may leave applicants on pathways below their professional trajectory. The paper is diagnostic: it identifies and characterises the recognition gap and its potential costs without prescribing how states should redesign their screening systems.

01Introduction

Taiwan became a super-aged society in 2025, with more than a fifth of its population aged sixty-five or older. A shrinking and ageing workforce has made the question of how the country sustains its labour supply increasingly urgent, and talent migration is one of the levers it has reached for. Taiwan is not alone in this. Other East Asian countries like Japan and South Korea, as well as other OECD economies under similar demographic pressure, compete for skilled migrants through comparable instruments. How a state decides whom to admit as skilled is therefore not a narrow administrative question but a central one for its economic future.

This paper examines Taiwan's talent governance — specifically, how its migration regime screens applicants for its skilled pathways. The system spans both the recruitment of high-skilled professionals and the import of lower-skilled labour. The concern of this paper is the former, as well as the eligibility criteria that decide who counts as a skilled migrant in the first place.

At a glance, these pathways look meritocratic: a place is offered to anyone who satisfies the criteria, regardless of background. On closer inspection, the criteria share a common feature. They screen on retrospective proxies — a certain level of prior salary, the prestige of a credential, a nationality or passport — each of which records what an applicant has already attained. These are understandable choices for a state trying to reduce uncertainty about whom it admits. But the same variables cannot register prospective value: the plausible but not-yet-resolved trajectory of someone on the way to becoming a high-skilled professional, whose outcome has not yet settled into a verifiable signal. This paper calls the resulting blind spot “the recognition gap.” It is a problem of classification, not of difficulty. The issue is not simply that a pathway is difficult to reach, but that the screening criteria do not directly evaluate this population during the interval in which their professional trajectory remains unresolved.

To examine whether this gap is particular to Taiwan or reflects a more general feature of skilled-migration screening, the paper compares Taiwan's talent governance with Canada. Canada was selected as a deliberately different institutional configuration: its economic-migration architecture includes a mature points-based system, provincial selection, and pathways through which experience accumulated within the destination can become migration-relevant. The comparison does not treat the two systems as opposites or claim that two cases can establish a general rule. Rather, it asks whether the same recognition problem persists despite meaningful differences in where, when, and how completed attainment becomes legible. If it does, retrospective screening becomes a plausible common mechanism rather than an explanation specific to Taiwan's policy design.

The contribution of this paper is to identify and characterise a pattern of talent governance that recurs across differently-positioned skilled-migration systems. It is a diagnostic exercise. It names the pattern and identifies its potential costs; it does not propose a redesign of either country's system.

02Taiwan

Taiwan is facing increasing workforce pressures associated with population ageing and low birth rates. In 2025, Taiwan officially became a super-aged society, further intensifying policy attention to talent attraction and workforce sustainability.

Taiwan has consequently developed several pathways for attracting, employing, and retaining foreign talent. The pathways most relevant to this paper include the Employment Gold Card; employer-sponsored professional work and related skilled-labour pathways; entrepreneurship and investment routes; and post-study pathways for foreign and Overseas Chinese graduates. These pathways differ in whom they target, what evidence they recognise, and whether recognition depends on prior attainment, employer sponsorship, capital, or experience accumulated within Taiwan. The following section briefly maps these pathways before the analysis turns to their underlying screening logic.

Employment Gold Card

The Taiwan Employment Gold Card, also considered Taiwan's elite pathway, is managed under the Act for the Recruitment and Employment of Foreign Professionals (外國專業人才延攬及僱用法). The card combines four functions: a work permit, resident visa, alien resident card (ARC), and re-entry permit.

The Gold Card's eligibility routes rely predominantly on documented prior attainment. Its most general income route requires evidence of monthly earnings of at least NT$160,000 within the last three years, verified through official documentation such as tax returns or assessments. Other professional routes similarly rely on combinations of completed education, years of experience, senior or executive status, recognised awards, successful entrepreneurship, publications, patents, or employment at internationally recognised institutions. Although the precise requirements differ by professional field and competent ministry, the underlying evidentiary structure is consistent: eligibility is established through achievements already accumulated and capable of documentary verification.

Standard Employment Pathways

Taiwan's standard employment pathways are regulated under three broad tiers of the Employment Service Act (就業服務法): 1) Foreign Professionals (外國專業人才) or White Collar Tier, 2) Foreign Labor / Migrant Workers (外國勞工 / 移工) or Blue Collar Tier, and 3) Foreign Skilled Workers (外國技術人員) or the Intermediate Skilled tier.

Taiwan's standard foreign professional-work pathway is employer-sponsored. Unlike the Gold Card, recognition therefore depends not only on the applicant's qualifications but also on the employing firm satisfying the regulatory requirements for hiring a foreign professional. For a domestic company established for less than one year, the employer must meet one of several qualifying thresholds, including paid-in capital of at least NT$5 million or turnover of at least NT$10 million. For a company established for more than one year, the qualifying criteria include turnover of at least NT$10 million in the most recent year or an annual average of at least NT$10 million over the preceding three years, alongside alternative criteria based on import-export performance or commission income.

The applicant must separately satisfy the applicable professional qualifications and salary requirements, while the employer applies for the work permit on the applicant's behalf. Recognition through this pathway therefore depends on both sides of the employment relationship: an applicant whose profile meets the professional criteria and an employer that is willing and legally eligible to translate that profile into a qualifying job offer.

Investment and Entrepreneurship Pathways

Taiwan also maintains entrepreneurship and investment-based residence routes, which recognise demonstrated capital, business formation, innovation credentials, or subsequent commercial performance. As these routes allocate residence principally through capital and entrepreneurial criteria rather than the professional-screening problem examined here, they are not analysed further.

Post-Study Pathway

Taiwan provides several post-study pathways for foreign and Overseas Chinese graduates, summarized in Table 1. These pathways differ primarily along two axes: where the applicant completed their education and the form of work authorization provided. They range from a standalone work permit without an employer requirement under the Top 200 pathway to permit-free work authorization for up to two years following graduation from a Taiwanese institution.

Under the 2025 amendment, foreign nationals and Overseas Chinese students graduating from a Taiwanese institution with an associate degree or higher may work without a work permit during a post-graduation extended residence of up to two years. During this period, they may work without an employer-specific work permit. This authorization is separate from the work permit required for students who work during their studies. The exemption is tied specifically to the graduate extended-residence status. If a graduate transitions during this period into formal employment under an employer-sponsored professional work permit (工作居留), the permit-free authorization ends. Leaving that employment does not restore the remaining period of permit-free work authorization; subsequent employment must instead proceed through the applicable employer-sponsored work-permit pathway.

For formal employment, foreign and Overseas Chinese graduates may also qualify through a points-based system under which the employer applies for the work permit and the graduate must obtain at least 70 of 200 points across eight weighted categories. These include education, offered salary, Chinese and foreign-language ability, work experience, employment in key industries or designated regions, and other recognised factors. A principal concession of this route is the waiver of the NT$47,971 minimum monthly salary threshold applicable to ordinary professional permits, lowering the entry barrier for entry-level graduate employment.

These post-study pathways therefore provide greater space for foreign graduates to accumulate experience within Taiwan before entering the standard employer-sponsored system. However, once a graduate transitions into the formal professional-work system, recognition again relies on observable indicators such as education, salary, language ability, accumulated experience, and employer sponsorship. The pathways consequently modify the timing and threshold of recognition without eliminating the retrospective structure of formal employment screening.

03Canada

Canada's economic migration architecture includes several federal, provincial, and post-study pathways. The pathways most relevant to this paper are Express Entry, which manages applications to three federal economic immigration programs through a points-based ranking system; the Provincial Nominee Program (PNP), through which provinces/territories nominate candidates according to regional priorities; and post-study pathways that allow eligible international graduates to accumulate Canadian work experience. Together, these pathways illustrate how Canada recognises both prior foreign attainment and human capital accumulated within Canada.

Express Entry

Express Entry is Canada's primary system for managing applications under three federal economic immigration programs: the Canadian Experience Class (CEC), Federal Skilled Worker Program (FSWP), and Federal Skilled Trades Program (FSTP). Eligibility for each program is based on completed and verifiable forms of attainment. The CEC principally recognises applicants who have accumulated qualifying skilled work experience within Canada, while the FSWP allows qualifying skilled work experience obtained abroad or in Canada to count alongside language and educational requirements. The FSTP similarly requires accumulated experience in an eligible skilled trade together with additional qualifying evidence, such as a valid job offer or Canadian certificate of qualification (Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, 2024b, 2026).

Eligible candidates enter the Express Entry pool and are ranked through the Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS), which assigns points for observable human-capital characteristics including age, education, official-language proficiency, Canadian work experience, skill-transferability factors, and other recognised attributes.

Additional points are available for specified factors, most notably 600 points for a provincial nomination. As of March 25, 2025, CRS points for arranged employment were removed, reducing the direct ranking value of a job offer while leaving job offers relevant to eligibility where particular programs require them (Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, 2024a).

In 2025, Canada also used category-based selection to prioritise candidates with specified language or occupational characteristics, while separately increasing invitations through the Canadian Experience Class as part of a policy emphasis on transitioning temporary residents already in Canada to permanent residence (Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, 2025).

Provincial Nominee Program (PNP)

The Provincial Nominee Program (PNP) allows participating provinces and territories to nominate candidates for permanent residence according to their own economic and labour-market priorities. This introduces a subnational layer of recognition alongside the federal system, allowing characteristics and labour-market needs identified by a particular province or territory to become migration-relevant through provincial selection.

PNP pathways can operate either through Express Entry or outside it. An Express Entry candidate who receives a provincial nomination is awarded an additional 600 CRS points, substantially increasing their likelihood of receiving an invitation to apply. Non-Express Entry streams allow candidates who meet the requirements of a provincial stream to obtain a nomination and subsequently apply to the federal government for permanent residence outside the Express Entry selection process (Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, 2018).

Quebec operates a separate immigration selection system and is outside the scope of this comparison.

Post-Study Pathway

Canada's Post-Graduation Work Permit (PGWP) provides eligible graduates of designated learning institutions with an open work permit, generally for a period determined by the level and duration of their study program and up to three years in qualifying cases. The pathway allows graduates to accumulate Canadian work experience that may subsequently become relevant to permanent-residence pathways, particularly the Canadian Experience Class.

Since November 2024, PGWP eligibility has become more selective. Applicants subject to the post-November 2024 requirements must demonstrate official language proficiency, generally at CLB/NCLC 7 for university programs and CLB/NCLC 5 for college, polytechnic, or other non-university programs. Certain non-degree programs are additionally subject to field-of-study requirements linked to designated areas of labour-market demand, while graduates with bachelor's, master's, or doctoral degrees are exempt from the field-of-study requirement (Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, 2024c).

The PGWP therefore does not itself constitute recognition for permanent residence. Rather, it provides a temporary period during which applicants can accumulate Canadian experience that may subsequently become legible within Canada's permanent-migration architecture. In particular, Canadian work experience can satisfy eligibility requirements under the CEC and contribute to an applicant's CRS ranking (Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, 2024c, 2026).

04Theory

This section addresses the concerns regarding the objectivity of what “skill” refers to. A substantial literature holds that “skill” is not a neutral fact but a social and political construction, where the lines that separate high-, middle-, and low-skilled are artificially created and carry the trace of race, gender, and nationality (Liu-Farrer et al., 2021).

This paper accepts that. It does not claim its instruments' categories are accurate or that “high-skilled” means anything real about a person. The claim is narrower: it concerns only the filter the state actually applies — what it treats as the mark of the elite skill — not whether that filter is valid.

For the argument, it is enough: whatever these categories are, states operationalise them, and the operationalisation has consequences regardless of the intent. A salary threshold of minimum NTD 160,000 or global accolades requirements in Taiwan policy creates a sorting procedure of real people into real outcomes, whether the category behind it is valid. The recognition gap is a property of that sorting. A filter can be stable and consequential without being valid — it can sort people decisively even if its categories are not true — and it is that behaviour this paper examines, not whether “high-skilled” objectively holds.

Recognising what “skilled” refers to is a second concern, separate from the construction problem. A state still has to screen for it using what it can observe. Macaluso's (2022) review of skill-based immigration policy makes the difficulty apparent: the labels a country screens on are specific socio-economic characteristics and labour-market demands. What the state actually seems to want is not on that list. Ability, motivation, adaptability, and long-term success are unobservable at the point of selection, and the literature Macaluso (2022) surveys finds that skill-based systems have very little ability to affect these in the receiving country. She concludes that while these systems may increase the share of educated migrants, their primary goal of improving migrant quality can still fail.

In this comparison, the recognition gap is treated as potentially structural rather than as a defect specific to Taiwan. If the problem arose principally from features unique to Taiwanese policy design, its persistence under a substantially different screening architecture would be less expected. Macaluso's (2022) account helps explain why it may nevertheless recur: skill-based migration systems necessarily rely heavily on characteristics that are observable at the point of selection.

Canada scores on its own observables — including Canadian experience, language bands, and provincial labour-market demand — and can therefore recognise forms of attainment that Taiwan's high-skilled pathways do not necessarily privilege in the same way. Yet an unresolved trajectory remains difficult to recognise until it resolves into observable evidence. The two systems differ in where and how retrospective value becomes legible, but both ultimately require that value to resolve into observable evidence before it can be recognised.

Taiwan's own policy debate sheds some light on the gap. In their review of the foreign-talent policy, Xin and Liu (2023) divide the system into three brackets: senior professionals (高階專業人才), intermediate-skilled workers (中階技術人力), and overseas-Chinese and foreign graduates (僑外生). Turning to the elite pathway, they ask whether the Employment Gold Card's NT$160,000 monthly salary floor should be relaxed to reach applicants they describe as outstanding talent whose records are shallower and whose salaries are slightly lower (資歷較淺、月薪稍低但優秀的人才). It is worth noting how the question is posed. Xin and Liu frame it as calibration — whether a threshold sits at the right level — rather than coverage. The people below the floor are seen, but they are seen as a parameter to be adjusted, not as a population the instrument cannot classify in the first place.

This paper takes the second view. The problem is not that the salary floor is set too high. It is that a retrospective salary screen cannot, by its structural design, register prospective value at whatever level the floor is set.

The principal survey source used for the Taiwanese case is the Gold Card Community Survey, an annual survey of Employment Gold Card holders administered by the National Development Council (NDC). The 2023 round drew 1,021 respondents from more than sixty countries, and it is the most direct available picture of who holds the card and why they stay. It has to be read within its limitations. The survey is self-selected, administered by the agency that issues the card, and conducted in English, so it reaches an engaged, anglophone subset of holders rather than a representative sample. More importantly for this paper, by its design, it can only see people who already hold the card, and hence, already cleared the screen. It says nothing about the person who never applied.

That last limit is not only a caveat. It is an instance of the argument. The population this paper is concerned with is defined partly by its absence from exactly this kind of record. A survey of cardholders cannot register the mobile person who never applies because that person is not in the survey. The data can therefore speak to the talent Taiwan does select and retain, and it is used here only for that. It does not function as evidence about the bracket. Where the bracket appears in this paper, it appears by structural inference, not by survey.

In Taiwan's domestic higher-education discourse, a parallel analysis of the country's talent-cultivation policy, Huang (2020) argues that the policy ideas of institutional “classification” and “the pursuit of excellence” formed the ideologies of the “top-tier” (頂尖) and “first-class” (一流) university. These categories were produced by policy discourse rather than neutral descriptions of standing. It is cited here only as a parallel example of how policy categories can construct administratively consequential classifications. Huang's subject is the cultivation of domestic talent (人才培育), a separate policy domain from the recruitment and retention of foreign professionals (人才延攬／留才) examined in this paper.

05Research Design

This paper is a desk-based, comparative study of skilled-migration screening in Taiwan and Canada. Taiwan is the focal case and Canada a deliberately dissimilar comparator. The comparison follows a most-different-systems logic, but is used diagnostically rather than as a strategy of causal identification. The purpose is to test whether a recognition problem initially identified in Taiwan persists under a substantially different institutional configuration.

Canada was selected because its economic-migration architecture differs from Taiwan's in several respects relevant to the comparison. It combines a mature federal points-based system with provincial selection and post-study pathways through which both foreign attainment and experience accumulated within Canada can become migration-relevant. Taiwan, by contrast, relies on a different combination of employer-sponsored professional work, high-skilled pathways, and post-study routes. These differences make Canada a useful comparator for asking whether the recognition gap depends on features particular to Taiwan or can persist across different ways of organising skilled-migration screening.

The comparison does not assume that difference between the cases eliminates rival explanations. Nor can two cases establish that retrospective screening is a general cause of the recognition gap. Its inferential value is narrower: if substantially different systems reproduce the same difficulty in recognising an advancing trajectory before it is resolved into observable evidence, explanations tied only to features unique to one system become less sufficient, while their shared reliance on completed and verifiable attainment becomes a plausible common mechanism.

Canada was also selected because its skilled-migration architecture is extensively documented in official policy materials and existing scholarship, allowing the comparison to be traced across federal, provincial, and post-study pathways. The resulting design therefore combines institutional difference with sufficient documentary evidence to examine how each system makes professional value administratively legible.

06Positionality

The question behind this paper began in personal experience. As a foreign professional navigating Taiwan's labour market and its migration pathways — through online applications and in-person job fairs — I noticed a recurring mismatch between the credentials and experience an applicant holds and what the system is able to read. My own position sits on the disadvantaged side of that screen: a record earned in one country does not transfer cleanly into how another country's instruments score it. I understand the individual reasons my own case does not translate. What inspired this research is that those reasons do not, on their own, account for how the policy translates it.

I disclose this perspective because it shapes the work in two ways. It sensitised me to a mechanism that is easy to miss from a position where the screen reads favourably, and it gives me a stake in the conclusion, which is a source of potential bias. The argument rests on each country's own legal texts, published statistics, and existing scholarship, and on a comparison with Canada chosen precisely so the account cannot rest on Taiwan alone — or on my own experience. Personal observation is the origin of the question, not evidence for its answer; no part of the analysis rests on my individual case.

The limits of this standpoint should also be stated. It is a single vantage, shaped by one nationality's experience of the system, and it does not stand in for the range of people this paper describes.

07Analysis

To better compare talent governance between Taiwan and Canada, it is crucial to first define precisely who the paper is and is not about, because the failure this paper identifies is easily mistaken for the ordinary friction of migration. The claim is about a structural blind spot in how skilled-migration regimes classify people, instead of the difficulty of immigrating.

This paper focuses on a “recognition problem”, rather than a “friction problem”. The issue is not simply that a pathway may be costly, slow, or difficult to reach, but that the criteria used for skilled-migration recognition do not directly evaluate an advancing professional trajectory while its outcome remains unresolved. During this interval, additional effort may eventually produce the retrospective markers the system can recognise, but the trajectory itself is not yet legible as such.

Three groups are excluded by definition. First is the obviously qualified, as the filter already catches them. The second is those with no plausible upward trajectory, who fall outside the bracket because this paper is not an argument that everyone deserves admission. The last one is the generic disadvantage or hardship, as the working paper bracket definition is bounded by indeterminacy of outcome, not by struggle.

What remains is the paper's subject: people who already possess relevant professional capabilities and evidence of an advancing trajectory toward high-skilled status, but whose trajectory has not yet resolved into the retrospective markers required for recognition. Membership in this bracket therefore depends on the trajectory rather than on belonging to a particular social or occupational group. Career pivoters, diaspora returnees, and deskilled-on-arrival professionals may fall within it where their existing capabilities and direction of development are plausible, but their classification remains unresolved at the point of screening.

As the preceding sections established, Taiwan and Canada organise skilled-migration recognition through substantially different institutional configurations. Placed side by side, however, their instruments share a structural feature: both rely heavily on records of completed and verifiable attainment, such as prior salary, conferred credentials, or accumulated experience, rather than directly evaluating an advancing professional trajectory while its outcome remains unresolved. The systems differ in which forms of attainment they recognise, where those attainments are accumulated, and how they become migration-relevant; what they share is the requirement that professional value become observable before it can be formally recognised.

For both Taiwan and Canada, this screen on completed attainment in itself is not evidence of poor design. Retrospective proxies are attractive to a risk-minimising state for reasons that are easy to state and hard to dismiss. Requirements such as prior proven salary, a conferred degree, a granted patent, or a documented year of Canadian experience are observable, cheaper to verify, and difficult to fabricate; each converts a complex judgement about a person's perceived value into the easily justified, easily audited signal.

A prospective judgment, by contrast, offers fewer of these administrative conveniences. It would require the state to assess unresolved trajectories, absorb the cost of mistaken predictions, and justify necessarily more subjective determinations than those based on completed and documentable attainment.

Prospective value does not refer to applicants who have yet to develop relevant skills or professional capacity. The bracket concerns applicants whose existing capabilities and advancing trajectories plausibly place them on a high-skilled professional path, but whose development has not yet resolved into the retrospective markers through which migration systems recognise that status. The defining uncertainty is therefore not simply whether the applicant possesses relevant capacity, but how an already advancing trajectory will ultimately resolve and whether it has become administratively legible at the point of screening.

Faced with this asymmetry, screening on what an applicant has already attained is a rational choice. The choice can be understood as an allocation of error. A system calibrated to completed attainment reduces the risk of false positives — admitting someone whose future contribution does not justify the initial classification — because completed achievements provide stronger observable signals than unresolved trajectories. But this comes with a corresponding risk: applicants with existing capabilities and plausible high-skilled trajectories may remain unrecognised because those trajectories have not yet resolved into the signals required for classification.

This risk is not randomly distributed. It is concentrated, by construction, among the prospective-value bracket. The claim is not that every applicant within this bracket would eventually become a successful high-skilled professional. Their eventual outcomes remain uncertain. Rather, retrospective screening has limited means to distinguish among them while their trajectories remain unresolved because the variables it reads are precisely those they have not yet accumulated.

In this way, the recognition gap is not evidence of an incompetent state. It is a concentrated and largely unrecognised cost of a justifiable risk-minimising design. The relevant trade-off is therefore not between certainty and uncertainty, but between different ways of managing uncertainty about future contribution.

The difference deferred above can now be stated directly. Both regimes screen on completed attainment, but they differ in where and how that attainment becomes migration-relevant. Taiwan's high-skilled pathways place substantial weight on achievements already established at the point of application, including prior salary, recognised credentials, and accumulated professional experience. Its post-study pathways create some room for in-country accumulation, but subsequent recognition still depends on completed characteristics that can be documented and scored.

Canada provides a different configuration. Foreign education and work experience can contribute to eligibility under federal programs, while Canadian education and work experience create additional routes through which applicants can strengthen their position within the migration system. The Canadian Experience Class makes completed Canadian work experience directly relevant to eligibility, while the CRS also rewards observable human capital characteristics and other recognised forms of attainment. Canada therefore shifts some of the timing and location through which applicants can become legible without replacing retrospective recognition itself.

The comparative significance lies not in an inversion between origin-country and host-country attainment, but in the persistence of the same recognition problem across these different configurations. Both systems provide multiple routes through which completed achievement can become legible, yet neither directly evaluates an advancing professional trajectory before it has resolved into the observable evidence its instruments can verify. The recognition gap therefore persists despite differences in where, when, and through which pathway retrospective value becomes recognisable.

That shared structure can generate two distinct downstream outcomes, shaped in part by the applicant's effective exit options. Where viable alternatives exist, the recognition gap may contribute to a leak; where exit is constrained, it may instead contribute to a trap. Effective exit options are not the only factor shaping what follows, but they provide the organising distinction used in this framework. The distinction also affects observability: a leak may leave little administrative trace, while a trap can remain visible as continued presence below trajectory.

Where the excluded applicant is mobile — holding, for example, the credentials, network, or capital that make alternative destinations viable — the recognition gap can produce a ‘leak’. Becoming legible to a retrospective screen requires further accumulation of the signals the system can verify: additional experience, higher prior earnings, recognised credentials, or other completed attainment. For a mobile applicant, pursuing those signals carries an opportunity cost. The relevant calculation is therefore not simply whether the destination is attractive, but whether the expected value of remaining in its recognition pipeline justifies the additional cost of becoming legible to it relative to available alternatives. Where that calculation is unfavourable, the applicant has an incentive to redirect effort elsewhere rather than wait for their trajectory to become recognisable.

This form of leak may occur before formal rejection and, in some cases, before an application is made at all. It is therefore difficult to observe directly in migration data: someone who considers a pathway but redirects their effort before entering it leaves little administrative trace. The leak is consequently an inferred consequence of the recognition gap rather than an outcome whose prevalence this study directly establishes. The claim here is narrower: retrospective screening can shape not only who qualifies, but also for whom entering or remaining in the recognition pipeline is worth the required investment.

On the other hand, where the excluded applicant is anchored — by family, cost, licensing constraints, or the sunk cost of relocation — the recognition gap can produce the opposite outcome: a ‘trap’. If the applicant cannot become legible to the skilled pathway corresponding to their professional trajectory but cannot or does not exit the destination, remaining may require them to rely on another legally available status or occupational pathway. The migration regime therefore does not by itself determine the applicant's subsequent employment outcome, but it constrains the set of legal trajectories available to them. Where the available alternatives do not correspond to their existing capabilities or professional direction, the applicant may remain below trajectory.

Unlike the leak, this outcome can leave an observable trace in the form of underemployment or deskilling. Such outcomes may also reflect licensing barriers, employer decisions, credential recognition, language, networks, and wider labour-market conditions; the claim here is therefore not that retrospective screening independently causes brain waste. Rather, the recognition gap can contribute to it by preventing the applicant's intended professional trajectory from becoming legally recognisable and thereby narrowing the pathways through which that trajectory can continue.

The two outcomes share a root but diverge according to the applicant's effective exit options: the leak appears as absence, while the trap appears as continued presence below trajectory (Table 3).

The two outcomes summarised in Table 3 map loosely onto familiar vocabulary without reducing to it. The trap maps onto what the migration literature calls brain waste, where skills are underused in the destination (Batalova et al., 2016); here it refers specifically to cases in which the recognition gap constrains the pathways through which existing capabilities can be professionally realised. The leak resembles brain drain, but where classic brain drain describes a country losing its own established talent to emigration, the leak describes a destination failing to attract or retain prospective talent before that talent becomes legible to its screening instruments. The distinction between them therefore turns not on skill level but on the applicant's effective exit options.

The persistence of the recognition problem across the two countries supports this paper's central comparative inference. Taiwan and Canada differ substantially in institutional architecture, demographic context, economic position, and migration history.

Moreover, as the previous section showed, the two systems differ in where and how recognised attainment is accumulated. Taiwan's high-skilled pathways place considerable weight on attainment already established at the point of application, while Canada's architecture provides routes through which attainment accumulated within the destination, particularly Canadian work experience, can become migration-relevant evidence. The contrast is not absolute: Canada also recognises foreign attainment, and Taiwan contains pathways in which in-country experience matters. What is significant is that the recognition gap persists across these different configurations. This makes the retrospective structure of screening a plausible common mechanism: although the systems differ in which completed achievements they recognise and where those achievements are accumulated, both require prospective value to resolve into observable evidence before it becomes legible to the state.

This is a diagnostic convergence and not a causal proof. While the two cases, however dissimilar, cannot on their own establish a general law, what they can do is locate where a shared failure is unlikely to originate. The comparison was, moreover, a genuine test rather than a foregone conclusion: had Canada's different screening architecture recognised prospective value in ways that Taiwan's did not, the gap would have appeared more policy-contingent, and this paper's structural claim would be correspondingly weaker. That it did not is consistent with Macaluso's (2022) account: points-based selection necessarily places substantial weight on characteristics that are observable at the point of assessment — such as education, income, language ability, and accumulated experience — while the defining feature of an unresolved trajectory is precisely that some of its future value has not yet become observable. The persistence of the recognition gap under Canada's different institutional configuration is therefore consistent with the mechanism proposed here.

The underlying screening structure is also unlikely to be unique to these two cases. Xin & Liu (2023) note that Japan's Highly-Skilled Professional and Korea's E-7 systems similarly rely on retrospective thresholds. These cases are not analysed here and therefore cannot establish the presence or consequences of a recognition gap, but they suggest that the reliance on completed attainment identified in Taiwan and Canada extends beyond the two cases examined in this study.

What this structural reading does and does not claim, and what follows from it, occupies the remainder of the analysis.

Two limits bound the claim before its implications are drawn. The argument characterises a pattern; it does not impute intent — neither state was shown to design its screen to exclude, only to have chosen instruments that function to exclude. Nor does it prescribe a redesign: naming the cost of a trade-off is not the same as showing the trade-off should be made differently, which would demand a cost-benefit analysis this paper does not attempt.

What the structural reading identifies is a cost that neither regime's screening instruments directly register, and that cost has two potential forms. Where the excluded applicant has viable exit options, the recognition gap can produce a leak. The relevant loss is not necessarily to a destination better able to recognise prospective value. Rather, a mobile applicant may redirect their effort toward an alternative whose expected opportunities better justify the costs of becoming legible to its migration system. In this sense, retrospective screening can impose a competitive cost before formal selection occurs: applicants whose trajectories remain unresolved may leave the recognition pipeline before their value becomes observable to the destination at all.

Where the excluded applicant is anchored, the corresponding cost takes the form of a trap: capabilities remain present in the destination but may be realised below the applicant's professional trajectory. Unlike the leak, this cost can accumulate over time.

Prolonged underemployment may also weaken the trajectory that initially remained unresolved. The scarring effects of unemployment and underemployment — including persistent wage and employment penalties — are well documented (Filomena, 2024), with skill atrophy one plausible channel. Among deskilled immigrants specifically, credential non-recognition has also been associated with psychological distress (Grant & Nadin, 2007). These effects do not establish that the recognition gap independently causes subsequent deskilling, but they suggest that an initial pathway constraint can become self-reinforcing: remaining below trajectory may progressively weaken some of the capabilities, experience, and labour-market position through which that trajectory could later become professionally realised.

The two costs are not symmetric in how they are distributed. In a leak, the principal cost identified by this framework falls on the destination, which loses a potentially valuable applicant from its effective talent pipeline, while the individual may redirect their trajectory toward another available option. In a trap, by contrast, the applicant remains within the destination but may bear more directly the costs of prolonged mismatch between existing capabilities and the pathways through which those capabilities can be professionally realised. Where that mismatch persists, the cumulative effects described above may make subsequent recovery more difficult. The distinction therefore concerns not only whether prospective talent leaves or remains, but also where the costs of the recognition gap are most directly experienced.

These two outcomes are not equally observable. Underemployment and deskilling among immigrants are documented phenomena, making the conditions associated with a potential trap more visible, although the specific contribution of the recognition gap to those outcomes remains inferential. The leak is harder to observe by construction: applicants who redirect their effort before applying, or before becoming formally excluded, may leave little administrative trace. This evidentiary asymmetry is consistent with the mechanism proposed here, but it also limits what this study can claim. In particular, this analysis identifies leak and trap as plausible downstream consequences of the recognition gap; it does not establish their prevalence or attribute observed cases of underemployment exclusively to migration screening.

This paper's contribution is not to resolve the recognition gap, but to identify and characterise it as a structural tendency of retrospective skilled-migration screening and to distinguish the two potential costs that can follow from it. The comparison suggests that the gap can persist across substantially different institutional configurations because both ultimately require an advancing trajectory to resolve into observable evidence before it becomes legible to the state. Whether these costs justify a different allocation of screening risk is a separate normative and policy question that this paper leaves open.

08Conclusion

This paper set out to examine how skilled-migration systems recognise professional value, beginning with Taiwan and testing the resulting argument through comparison with Canada. It identifies a consistent blind spot in screening systems that rely predominantly on completed and verifiable attainment. Applicants may already possess relevant professional capabilities and an advancing high-skilled trajectory while lacking the retrospective markers through which that trajectory becomes administratively legible. This is the recognition gap: the interval in which existing capabilities and professional direction are plausible, but have not yet resolved into the observable evidence required for formal recognition.

Placing Taiwan beside Canada suggests that the recognition gap is unlikely to be a Taiwanese peculiarity. The two systems differ in which forms of completed attainment they recognise, where those attainments are accumulated, and how they become migration-relevant. Canada provides more explicit routes through which experience accumulated within the destination can become relevant to subsequent migration status, while Taiwan places greater weight on attainment already established at particular points of formal recognition. Yet across both configurations, an advancing trajectory must ultimately resolve into observable evidence before it becomes legible to the state. Two cases cannot establish a general rule, but the persistence of the recognition gap across these different arrangements makes retrospective screening a plausible common mechanism rather than a feature unique to either system.

The recognition gap can carry two potential downstream costs, shaped in part by an applicant's effective exit options. Where viable alternatives exist, a mobile applicant may redirect their effort rather than continue investing in becoming legible to a particular migration system, producing a potential leak from the destination's talent pipeline that may leave little administrative trace. Where exit is constrained and the applicant remains, the gap may instead contribute to a trap by narrowing the pathways through which existing capabilities can be professionally realised. Prolonged mismatch may then interact with other labour-market barriers, and in some cases, become self-reinforcing. These outcomes are not equally observable, nor does this study establish their prevalence, but together they identify two ways in which the costs of retrospective recognition can extend beyond the formal decision to admit or exclude.

This paper has characterised a pattern; it has not solved it. The comparison suggests that the recognition gap can persist across different configurations of skilled-migration screening because unresolved professional trajectories remain difficult to classify until they become observable through completed attainment. Reducing that gap would require accepting a different distribution of uncertainty and screening error, with costs of its own. Whether those costs justify a different allocation of risk is a normative and policy question beyond the scope of this study. The contribution here is narrower: to make the recognition gap, and the potential costs associated with it, analytically visible.
