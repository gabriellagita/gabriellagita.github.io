Why these constraints?

Paid, pre-booked and small are proposed design choices, not settled facts. Each is a hypothesis with its own evidence and its own open test.

Read the four design decisions

Tainan knows what it’s proud of.Does anyone outside it read it?

Pride is made of context that insiders carry without noticing. Legibility is what survives when that context is taken away.

Food, temples, history, culture. Those words tell a visitor what exists. They don’t say why this version is worth their limited time. From a year in Taiwan, my own observation, not a survey: ask people what to see in Tainan and the answer is usually food and temples.

So the question isn’t how to persuade visitors that heritage matters. It’s how to make something locally meaningful specific enough that the right visitor can decide whether it’s worth their attention. Not everyone has to care.

Begin with what the visitor already recognizes.End somewhere they couldn’t have found.

Tainan Pairings is a proposed small, paid, pre-booked cultural experience. It’s a pilot concept. It doesn’t exist yet and can’t be booked.

Temple→Object→Question→Maker→Context

Instead of asking someone to care about traditional craftsmanship, a pairing starts with the temple in front of them. The object raises a question. The maker’s workshop becomes relevant because it answers something the visitor is already curious about.

One object.One question.One relationship.

The test is simple. Can the visitor later explain, in their own words, why the thing they met matters?

Design decision 03 / Why one question?
Decision
Build each pairing around one question that comes from encountering the object.
Reason
A broad category such as “traditional craft” gives a visitor no particular reason to stop. A specific question gives them a threshold: something they became curious about at the object, which the maker can then answer. The aim is not twenty facts. It is that the visitor can later explain, in their own words, why the thing matters.
Evidence

Published

  • No research in the evidence set supports this directly. The nearest comparator is Japan’s tourism agency, which presents “gastronomy tourism” as food tied to local ingredients, customs and traditions that differ from region to region, rather than food in general.R12 That shows a category-to-specificity approach exists. It does not show it works.

Project inference

This is hypothesis H3: a specific question may create more useful curiosity than a broad category.

Test
Whether visitors can explain the object’s significance afterwards in their own words, and which questions open curiosity and which do not.

The object is ornate.The question is how it holds together.

The worked example is 永川大轎 ↔ 金華府. It’s illustrative: it shows how a pairing works. 永川大轎 is not a pilot partner, and nobody there has been contacted.

It shows the logic because the thing a visitor sees, an ornate temple palanquin, rests on production knowledge that’s easy to miss.

Object
Palanquin (神轎), the kind of object a visitor sees at a temple.
Maker
永川大轎. Wang Yongchuan (1932–2020) learned carpentry from his father and developed his own palanquin-making technique. The workshop now runs across three generations.S2S4S5
Technique
Mortise-and-tenon joinery, with no nails.S1S2S4 The National Cultural Memory Bank adds that the tenoned joints are then coated with water-based glue.S3 So the claim is “no nails”, not “no glue”.
Reported scale
Reported to have made nearly seven in ten of the old city’s temple palanquins.S2 That is a reported figure, not a measured one.
Location
The workshop moved to Annan District in 2022, with a display area and planned experience activities.S4 Older sources place it on Shennong Street in the old city.S5 Whether any visitor access remains there is unconfirmed.
Pair
金華府, as an illustrative temple. This page makes no claim that the temple uses the maker’s palanquins.
Distance
Not established. How a visitor would get from the temple to the maker is an open question.
Founding year
Sources disagree (1952, 1955 and 1967 all appear), so none is given.S1S3S5

No nails.So what holds this together?

The answer isn’t only joinery. Wang Yan, the third generation, points to material selection, planing, proportion and joining, and to a low centre of gravity that reduces damage from sway in processions.S4 Wang Yongchuan’s own explanation, as reported in a travel feature, is that nails are quicker but work loose, while a tenon joint is harder to make and can be tightened again when it loosens.S6

That is the legibility change the opening tries to show. An ornate religious object gets one detail that gives the visitor a reason to see how it was made.

A visit would be arranged with the maker in advance. Whether they would host visitors at all, and on what terms, is unknown.

Design decision 04 / Why pre-booked?
Decision
Visits are booked ahead by small groups. There is no drop-in.
Reason
These are working businesses, not attractions. A visit needs the maker’s agreement and a number of people the workshop can handle. Booking ahead also asks visitors to commit time and planning.
Evidence

Published

  • No research in the evidence set addresses pre-booking directly. The closest is adjacent: the Amsterdam–The Hague study describes redistribution as being about linking products and sharing benefits,R6 and the Kyoto work treats readiness to receive visitors as something to assess.R8

Project inference

Pre-booking, group limits, practitioner agreement and pricing together act as demand management for the host (hypothesis H6).

Test
Whether booking ahead brings in the right visitors or puts them off, and whether hosts found the volume manageable.

Design decision 02 / Why 5–7?
Decision
Limit each group to about five to seven people.
Reason
A working workshop can only reasonably receive so many people at once, so the group is sized to what the host and place can receive, not to maximum volume. In a small group everyone can hear and ask, and visitors can talk to each other as well as to the guide. “Small” is not claimed to be sustainable on its own.
Evidence

Published

  • A conceptual paper sets out frameworks and a typology for how destinations develop, concentrate, diversify and link their tourism products, including niche and mass products and thematic and spatial synergies. It treats diversification, intensification and linkage as potentially important for competitiveness and sustainable development (Benur & Bramwell 2015). It does not test whether spreading visitors out is sustainable.R5
  • A study of the Amsterdam–The Hague agreement to redistribute visitors found it only moderately successful, and described redistribution as being about linking products and sharing benefits.R6
  • Amsterdam’s 2025 neighbourhood monitoring found six neighbourhoods with carrying capacity under pressure, one more than in 2023, and the list of neighbourhoods changed. It does not show that dispersal caused the change.R7
  • Research on alternative attractions in Kyoto combines supply, observed visitor movement, experience quality and accessibility.R8

Project inference

Spreading visitors out is not assumed to be sustainable. That caution comes from the two Amsterdam sources above, not from Benur and Bramwell. A local asset existing does not make it a visitor-ready product. Group limits, pre-booking and the maker’s agreement are how this pilot tries to match visitors to what a host can receive. This is hypothesis H6.

Test
Whether five to seven suited the host, and whether the visitor volume felt appropriate to them.

Not everyone should care.The right visitor should be able to tell.

Demand isn’t uniform. People become curious through food, architecture, ritual, making, design or history. A proposition doesn’t need universal appeal. It needs enough specificity for the right person to recognize whether it’s for them.

Three possible audiences for a pilot, all hypotheses:

  • Independent international visitors who have already chosen to spend time in Tainan.
  • Repeat visitors to Taiwan.
  • Foreign residents already living in Taiwan.

For the last two, familiarity can create its own problem: a destination can feel completed. A more specific proposition might make something familiar unfamiliar again. That is a hypothesis to test, not an established finding.

“Two days in Tainan” describes scarce visitor time. It says nothing about when a pairing would run.

Adjacent evidence: a 2025 study using mobile-phone data in Seoul, Busan and Jeju found that different international visitor groups concentrate spatially in different ways,R9 which is a reason not to treat “international visitors” as one market. Its nationality-specific findings aren’t carried over to Taiwan. Japan’s tourism agency runs separate campaigns for people who have never visited Japan and for repeat visitors.R12

Every constraint is a decision.Every decision is a hypothesis.

Four choices shape the proposed product. Each is set out in four parts. The evidence is adjacent: it grounds the reasoning without proving the pilot will work. Anything beyond it is a hypothesis, and the Test line says what would need to be learned.

Design decision 01 / Why paid?
Decision
Propose the pilot as a paid experience instead of defaulting to a free one. The product does not exist; this is a decision about the concept.
Reason
Free access is good at drawing people in, but participation is not the only question here. The pilot also asks whether the proposition is valuable enough for someone to commit money, time, planning and attention to it. The people whose work forms part of the experience also need to be paid for it.
Evidence

Published

  • Zero is not simply another low price: consumers can disproportionately favour free options (Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely).R4
  • In an online choice experiment with 536 valid responses from people in China, willingness to pay differed by interpretive attribute. Distinctive local culture raised it; long service length and interactivity lowered it. Four groups of tourists showed different preferences (Zhao & Chan 2023).R2
  • At one World Heritage site in China, interpersonal interpretation outperformed non-personal interpretation in delivering heritage values (Weng, Liang & Bao 2020). It measured perceived heritage value, not willingness to pay.R1
  • Willingness to pay for authenticity has been studied at Pingxi, Taiwan. No figure from it is carried over to this concept.R3

Project inference

Free can work for participation, but it cannot show whether the experience itself is valuable enough to commit to. Payment is used here as one observable form of commitment. It is not assumed to create value.

Test
Whether the relevant visitors will pay for this proposition at a viable price, and how its length and how hands-on it is change that. The price itself is the host’s decision.

A concept needs a host.This one doesn’t have one yet.

Nobody has agreed to anything. The structure below shows how a real version could be built, and it would still need confirming with the Tourism Administration and Tainan’s tourism bureau. It isn’t legal advice.

Three workable shapes

  • A travel agency partner holds the licence, the contract and the money. The concept supplies the content.
  • A university or city host runs it as a pilot and engages a licensed guide.
  • The craftspeople sell their own activity directly and keep the money. The project supplies framing and connection.

The proposed host is a university, with the city as a named partner. Neither has been approached. It’s written so that a university pilot works alone, with city involvement adding access and scale.

The guide

Foreign visitors would need to be received by a guide licensed in the relevant language. Students could take part as interpreters and hosts, not as guides for foreign visitors. This needs further confirmation with the Tourism Administration.

Compensation

Hosts, guides and the people who share their work would be fairly compensated and credited, and not only in financial terms. Pricing is the host’s decision, not this study’s.

What a real version would need to settle

  • Which makers would host visitors at all, on what terms, and how they are compensated.
  • How a visitor gets between the temple and the maker.
  • Whether a university would host, and whether the city would join.
  • The exact regulatory boundaries for a non-commercial, institution-hosted pilot.
  • Whether students can be credited or paid, and under what course structure.
  • Consent for recording practitioners, and for photographing sites that restrict it.
  • Which days each business is open, which may decide the route.
  • The ask to the host, which is deliberately still open.

Not whether they enjoyed it.Whether they can say why it matters.

Satisfaction, memorability, recommendation, willingness to pay, future curiosity and repeat behaviour are related but different outcomes. A five-star rating on the day can’t stand in for all of them.

A systematic review of memorable tourism experience research found the field’s theory fragmented. It recommended caution with the standard scale, attention to the limits of self-report, mixed methods, and including suppliers’ perspectives.R10 That is part of why the pilot’s questions go beyond a single rating and include the maker’s and host’s experience.

Questions the pilot would ask:

  • Did someone choose to book and pay?
  • What first triggered their curiosity?
  • What do they remember later?
  • Can they explain why the object or place matters?
  • Did the experience change what else they became curious about in Tainan?
  • Would they book another pairing?
  • What was it like for the maker or host?
  • Was the visitor volume right for the host?

The sharpest question: did the experience close the destination, or open another question?

The hypothesis is that specificity can make a destination feel less exhaustible, by revealing distinctions inside categories visitors thought they already understood. Research has linked novelty to memorable experiences and revisit intention.R11 That doesn’t show these pairings would raise repeat visits.

The project’s own hypotheses

These are design hypotheses for the pilot. They are not research findings. The sources give adjacent evidence only.

  • H1A locally meaningful asset can exist without being legible enough for an outsider to judge it as a tourism proposition.
  • H2A familiar attraction can be an effective way into a less familiar layer of the city.
  • H3A specific question may create more useful curiosity than a broad category such as “traditional craft”.
  • H4Revealing distinctions inside a familiar destination may create curiosity about what else is left to discover.
  • H5Some visitors may see enough value in practitioner access and interpretation to exchange money, time, planning and attention for it.
  • H6Small, pre-booked products may let visitor demand be matched to the capacity of particular hosts.
  • H7A portfolio of differentiated experiences may give more ways to place different visitors without turning every local asset into a mass attraction.

What this does not claim

  • That Taiwan tourism statistics prove demand for craft experiences.
  • That international tourists inherently want “authentic culture”.
  • That paid experiences are more valuable than free ones.
  • That interpretation automatically leads to purchasing.
  • That dispersal is automatically sustainable.
  • That Tainan Pairings would increase repeat visits to Taiwan.
  • That it solves Taiwan’s national tourism positioning.

The pilot asks something narrower: can a familiar attraction become the gateway to a less legible layer of the city, and can that layer become valuable enough for an appropriate audience to spend time and money exploring it?

Capacity decides the scale.The logic travels.

A destination needs enough different, legible experiences for different kinds of visitor demand to find an appropriate place to go, without every local asset becoming a mass attraction. A museum may receive hundreds of people. A commercial district may receive thousands. A working workshop may only reasonably receive six.

Put visitors where they have a reason to be, and where the place can actually receive them.

The pairings format is modular for that reason. Each can run at the frequency, size and price its host can support.

The pride-versus-legibility gap isn’t specific to Tainan. It exists wherever a place markets what it’s proud of rather than what reads from outside, and the pairing format is a structure that can be reused elsewhere.

About this project

An independent pilot concept. Any full implementation would require fair compensation and credit for everyone involved, including hosts, guides and the people who share their work, and not only in financial terms.

This is a speculative case study. It wasn’t commissioned, and nobody named in it has endorsed it or agreed to take part.
